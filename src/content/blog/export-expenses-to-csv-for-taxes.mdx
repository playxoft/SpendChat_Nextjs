There's a particular evening that happens once a year. Your accountant has asked for "your expenses for the tax year", you open your tracker, press Export, and receive a file containing every transaction you have ever entered, personal groceries included, in a column order nobody chose, which you now have to sort, filter and prune in a spreadsheet at 11pm.

That step is where the wrong rows get deleted. It happens most often when the stakes are highest.

Here's a workflow that skips it entirely. It takes about ten minutes, and once a quarter it takes two.

*(General guidance on getting the file out, not tax advice. The rules about what's deductible and how long to keep records are your jurisdiction's, and worth checking with someone who knows them.)*

## The ten-minute version

**1. Switch to the right profile.** If your business records live in a separate [profile](/features/multiple-profiles), select it. This is the single biggest source of contaminated exports. A file that mixes personal and business rows is worse than no file, because someone has to decide which is which.

**2. Set the date range.** Any two dates, not just whole months, so a tax year that starts on 6 April is one filter rather than an assembly job afterwards.

**3. Narrow further if you need to.** Type (expenses only), a single category, or a search term across titles and descriptions.

**4. Press CSV.** The download is the view you're looking at.

**5. Skim it once.** Open the file, check the totals block at the top against what you expected, and check the first and last rows are inside your date range. Thirty seconds, and it catches the wrong-profile mistake before your accountant does.

## Why filtering before you export expenses to CSV matters

Plenty of export buttons dump the database. The pitch is generosity, here's everything, but it moves the real work downstream to the least reliable moment.

Consider what you're actually doing at 11pm in a spreadsheet: sorting by date, selecting rows outside the range, deleting them, then doing the same for the personal profile, then discovering you deleted one row too many and having to re-download. Every one of those steps is a place to make an error you won't notice, in a document that will be filed with a tax authority.

An export that carries the filters removes the entire category of mistake. The rows in the file are the rows you were looking at, because you already narrowed them on screen where you could see what you were doing.

One thing that deliberately doesn't carry across: the sort. The file is always newest first, however you'd ordered the table. That's a small surprise the first time and the right default — a report with a stable, predictable order is easier to diff against last quarter's.

## What's in the file

Three details are worth understanding rather than just knowing, and one of them will otherwise cost you an afternoon.

**The totals are already there, so don't sum anything.** Rows 1 to 11 are a report block: the workspace, the profile, the date range you filtered to, then total income, total expense and the net between them. Row 12 is the column header and the transactions start at **row 13**. Whatever figure you were about to calculate is sitting at the top of the file already.

**If you do sum, sum column E and stop before the last row.** Amounts are signed — expenses negative, income positive — so arithmetic over them works, but two things trip people up. Amount is the fifth column, **E**; column F is the currency code, and summing it silently returns zero. And the table ends with a `Total:` row that repeats the net, so a range running to the bottom of the file counts it twice and hands you exactly double. `=SUM(E13:E512)` over the data rows is the honest version.

**The file names itself by the day you exported it** — `spendchat-2026-08-21.csv` — not by the range inside it. Two exports on the same day overwrite each other in your downloads folder, so rename the file to the range as you save it. The report block means the file still explains itself if you don't.

One more: it carries the transaction **title**, not the longer description, and anything you never categorised comes through as `Uncategorized` rather than blank. Titles are what you'd want in a report — so if the detail lives in your descriptions, that's a reason to put the important part in the title as you go.

## Opening cleanly in Excel

CSV is a format with a surprising number of ways to go wrong. Three choices here are deliberate:

**UTF-8.** Category names and merchant names contain accents, currency symbols and non-Latin scripts. Anything else mangles them.

**CRLF line endings.** What RFC 4180 specifies, and what Excel handles most predictably.

**Proper quoting.** Commas and quote marks inside your own text are escaped, so a title like `Dinner, split with Sam` doesn't shift every column to its right by one. This is the failure that produces a file that looks fine until row 340.

If your regional settings use a comma as the decimal separator, Excel's import dialog is the reliable route — open a blank sheet and use Data → From Text/CSV rather than double-clicking the file.

## When a PDF beats a CSV

A spreadsheet is for someone who's going to calculate. A PDF is for someone who's going to read, sign, or file.

Press Print — or ⌘P / Ctrl+P — and the page reformats itself into a report: sidebar, filters and buttons drop out, the table gains thin gridlines, and a header appears with the workspace, profile and date range. Choose *Save as PDF* and you have something you can attach to an email without apologising for it.

| Situation | Send | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accountant doing your return | CSV | They'll sort, sum and re-categorise |
| Reimbursement claim | PDF | It's read once and filed |
| Landlord or agency asking for proof | PDF | Fixed layout, obvious date range |
| Moving to another app | CSV | It's the only one that imports |
| Your own analysis | CSV | Pivot tables need rows |
| A record you want unchanged | PDF | Nobody edits it by accident |

## Three things that will surprise you once

**5,000 rows per file.** Beyond that, export a year at a time with the date filter. Most people never meet this; heavy business users will.

**Attachments don't come with it.** The CSV is the transactions. Receipts download individually from the [vault](/features/receipts-and-files), and there isn't a one-click archive of all of them yet. If your accountant needs the receipts too, a share link per file is the current route — and it's much easier if you [attached them as you went](/blog/how-to-organize-receipts-digitally).

**The export respects your access, not your intent.** In a shared [workspace](/features/workspaces) you export what you can see. If a colleague's profile isn't in your view, it isn't in the file — which is correct, and occasionally not what someone expected.

## What an accountant actually wants

If you're self-employed and sending this to someone else, a few minutes of preparation saves a round of emails.

**Two files, not one.** Income and expenses separately, each filtered by type. It's how they'll work with them anyway, and it means neither of you has to explain the signed-amount convention.

**Nothing personal in either.** Filter to the business [profile](/features/multiple-profiles) before exporting. If your books aren't split by profile, this is the argument for splitting them: it turns "which of these are business?" from an annual conversation into a filter. If someone else needs to see those books, [give them the profile rather than your password](/blog/how-to-track-shared-expenses-with-your-partner).

**Titles that name the counterparty.** "Invoice 114, Meridian" is a line an accountant can reconcile. "Payment received" is one they'll email you about. The title is what lands in the file, so it's worth the extra three words when you're logging.

**A note about what's missing.** If some receipts aren't attached, or a category is a guess, say so in the email. Accountants price uncertainty into their time; a two-line caveat is cheaper than them discovering it.

**Send the range you agreed.** Tax years don't always align to calendar years: 6 April in the UK, 1 July in Australia, 1 April in India, calendar in the US. The date filter takes any two dates precisely so you can match the one that applies to you rather than exporting twelve months and hoping.

An [analytics](/features/analytics) screenshot for the same range is a useful extra: it gives them the category shape at a glance, which is often how they spot the thing you forgot to claim.

## Do it quarterly

The annual scramble is a scheduling problem, not a tooling one. Once a quarter, spend two minutes: filter to the business profile and the last three months, export, save it in a folder named for the tax year, and skim the totals.

Three things come out of that habit. You catch missing transactions while you still remember them. You notice a mis-categorised row when fixing it takes five seconds. And come filing season the work is already done — four files, or one export over the full range as a cross-check.

## What a good export button tells you about a company

An export feature is a decent proxy for how a company thinks about your data, which is why it's worth checking before you commit a year of history rather than after.

Behind a paid tier means your history is hostage. PDF-only means it's technically yours and practically not. A dump of everything, unfiltered, means the work is yours regardless. None of those are accidents — each is a decision someone made about how easy leaving should be.
