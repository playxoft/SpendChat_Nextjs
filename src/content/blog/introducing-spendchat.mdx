Most people have tried to track their spending. Most people have stopped. Usually in the second week, usually without deciding to — the app just stopped getting opened, and by the time anyone noticed there was a gap too big to reconstruct.

SpendChat is a **free money tracker** built around that failure rather than around a feature list. This post is what it is, what it deliberately isn't, and the six decisions everything else follows from.

## The problem isn't reporting. It's the first ten seconds.

Every tracker can show you a pie chart. The chart is the easy part — it's arithmetic over rows you already have.

The hard part is getting the rows. Logging a coffee has to be quicker than the thought "I'll do it later", and every tracker that dies does so because it lost that race: a modal, six fields, a category dropdown that needs scrolling, a save button that navigates you somewhere you didn't ask to go.

So the first decision was to make entry feel like sending a message rather than filling in a form.

## The six decisions

**1. Entry is a conversation, not a form.** Transactions live in a feed. Income on one side, expenses on the other, each a bubble with its category and time, a running balance above and day dividers through it. The composer sits under the feed and is always open — no plus button, no modal to wait for; `#` tags a category from the title field; ⌘/Ctrl + Enter sends. No save dialog, no page change, nothing to dismiss. [More on the chat model.](/features/chat-expense-tracker)

**2. Four ways in, because your hands aren't always free.** Type it. Or write one sentence covering a whole run of errands and let [AI entry](/blog/how-to-track-expenses-with-ai) hand back a draft per purchase, each one editable. Or switch the composer to AI mode and hold `M` to [say it out loud](/blog/voice-expense-tracking-in-any-language), in whichever languages you actually mix. Or paste rows out of a spreadsheet into [bulk add](/features/bulk-add), 500 at a time. Same feed, same categories, four routes.

**3. No bank connection. At all.** There's no aggregator behind this and no screen that asks for banking credentials, so there's nothing to breach and nothing to reconnect after a bank security update. It also means cash isn't invisible: a market stall, a taxi paid in notes, the twenty you handed a neighbour all land the same way as a card payment. [The full argument.](/blog/expense-tracker-without-bank-connection)

**4. The AI drafts; you decide.** Both AI and voice entry stop at a review step. The model splits your sentence into rows with an amount, a date, a type and a category chosen from *your* list — and then waits. Nothing is written until you press the button. A model that files straight into your records is a model that quietly corrupts them, and you'd never find out.

**5. Your data leaves as easily as it arrives.** Filter the [transactions table](/features/transactions) to what you want, press CSV, and the file is the view you were looking at rather than a dump to trim. Or print, and the page reformats into a report your browser saves as a PDF. No paid tier and no watermark — the only ceilings are mechanical: 5,000 rows to a file, and a print carries the rows you have loaded. [What's in the file, and both caps in detail.](/blog/export-expenses-to-csv-for-taxes)

**6. Open source, so the claims are checkable.** All of it is on GitHub under the AGPL-3.0 — the auth checks, the queries, the AI prompts, the exporter. "We don't sell your data" is a promise; "there is no bank integration in this repository" is something you can verify in a minute. [How to audit any of it.](/blog/open-source-expense-tracker)

## What it deliberately isn't

A product is as much the things it refuses as the things it ships, and these refusals are all load-bearing.

**No budgets or goals — yet.** The honest reason is sequencing. A budget is a claim about the future built on a record of the past, and the record has to be trustworthy first. Ship budgets on top of a half-kept feed and you get a number that's wrong in a way nobody can see.

**No per-transaction currency.** Currency belongs to the workspace, not the row. Multi-currency sounds like a small feature and isn't: the moment two rows carry different units, every total needs an exchange rate with a date attached, and the "total" you show becomes a snapshot of a rate you didn't record. One currency per set of books means the numbers mean exactly what they say. If you genuinely run two currencies, that's two workspaces.

**No streaks, badges or nudges.** Gamification works on habits with no intrinsic payoff. This one has an obvious payoff, and a notification saying you haven't logged in three days is a notification you'll mute in a week.

**No ads, no data selling.** There's no version of this where the reader is the product.

## Under the hood, for the curious

Next.js on the App Router, Tailwind and shadcn/ui, Neon Postgres through Drizzle, Firebase Authentication, deployed to Cloudflare Workers via OpenNext with files in R2.

Two details that matter more than the stack does. **Money is stored as whole minor units** — 62.40 is the integer 6240, never a float — because floating-point arithmetic on money accumulates error that shows up as a balance being a cent out and nobody being able to say why. And **every read is scoped to your own access** in one place rather than checked ad hoc in each handler, which is the difference between a permission system and a collection of `if` statements that eventually disagree.

The AI model is configuration rather than code: no model id appears anywhere in the source, so a self-hosted instance can point at whichever provider it prefers, or at none.

## The first minute

Worth describing, because "set up in under a minute" is a claim everyone makes.

You sign in with Google, which puts you straight in, or with an email and password, which sends a verification link you have to click before the account opens — one trip through your inbox, and the only part of this that leaves the app. Either way it is handled by Firebase Authentication, so no password ever reaches our servers. That is the whole of setup: no onboarding wizard, no budget to define before you're allowed to type anything, and no connect-your-accounts step to skip.

What happens behind that is one workspace named after you, fifteen starter categories in it, and a default profile. The currency and number format are guessed from where you are — the country your connection reports, then your browser's language — and set on that first workspace only. An admin can change both later, and any workspace you create afterwards inherits whatever you're using at the time.

Then you land on the tracker: an empty feed, with the composer already open underneath it. The first thing to do is type a number.

That ordering is deliberate. Most finance apps front-load configuration because configuration is how they justify the account: budgets, goals, linked institutions, notification preferences. All of it happens before the app has a single transaction to reason about, which means every one of those decisions is made blind. It also gives you five chances to close the tab.

Everything here is set up *afterwards*, once you have data and a reason. Rename the categories once you've seen which ones you actually use. Add a second [profile](/features/multiple-profiles) the week you need to separate business from personal. Invite someone when there's something to share. None of it is required, and none of it stands between you and your first transaction.

## Who it's for

**People whose spending is partly cash.** Nothing that syncs from a bank will ever see it.

**People who've bounced off the big trackers.** If the reason was that logging took too long, this is the thing that's actually different.

**Freelancers and small businesses.** Separate profiles for business and personal, a per-profile grant for your accountant, and an export that carries your filters at tax time.

**Households.** One workspace, roles per person, every transaction showing who entered it — rather than [one shared login and no idea who spent what](/blog/how-to-track-shared-expenses-with-your-partner).

**People who'd rather read the code than a privacy policy.** It's all there.

It's free, setting it up is a sign-in and nothing after it, and there's nothing to disconnect if you change your mind.
